Nitric Oxide and Diet: How Beets and Leafy Greens Boost Blood Flow and Performance

A
Anthony Smith • Oct 15, 2026 • 7 min read

Before most people lace up their running shoes, they reach for a pre-workout powder. But research increasingly points to something sitting in the produce aisle as a more effective performance enhancer: nitrate-rich vegetables. The same compounds that make beets, arugula, and spinach vibrant are converted by your body into nitric oxide (NO)—a signaling molecule that relaxes blood vessels, lowers blood pressure, and delivers more oxygen to working muscles. Athletes from elite marathoners to recreational cyclists have begun using beet juice as a legal, food-based ergogenic aid. But the benefits extend far beyond sport. Nitric oxide is foundational to cardiovascular health, brain function, and immune regulation. If you have never paid attention to where your dietary nitrates come from, the science here will change your grocery list.

What Nitric Oxide Does Inside Your Body

Nitric oxide is a tiny, short-lived gas molecule produced in nearly every tissue. Its most celebrated role is vasodilation—widening blood vessels by relaxing their smooth muscle layer. When a vessel dilates, resistance drops and blood flow increases, delivering more oxygen and nutrients throughout the body. This mechanism is so central to cardiovascular biology that the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to the researchers who mapped it. Beyond circulation, NO regulates neurotransmission, mitochondrial efficiency, platelet aggregation, and immune defense against pathogens. Deficient NO signaling is linked to hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and accelerated atherosclerosis. Your body synthesizes NO through two major pathways: the enzymatic route (converting L-arginine via eNOS) and, increasingly recognized as critical, the dietary nitrate pathway—meaning what you put on your plate is a direct input into your vascular biology.

Research highlight: A 2016 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that dietary nitrate supplementation reduced systolic blood pressure by an average of 3.55 mmHg—a clinically meaningful reduction comparable to effects seen with some antihypertensive medications.

From Fork to Bloodstream: The Dietary Nitrate Pathway

When you eat a nitrate-rich vegetable, the conversion to NO follows a precise three-step cycle. First, nitrates (NO₃⁻) are absorbed in the small intestine and enter circulation. Up to 25% of dietary nitrate is actively secreted into saliva, where bacteria residing naturally on the back of the tongue reduce it to nitrite (NO₂⁻). Swallowing delivers that nitrite into the acidic stomach, where a fraction converts chemically to nitric oxide. This is the entero-salivary nitrate cycle—and it explains a surprising research finding: using antibacterial mouthwash immediately before eating beets significantly blunts the blood pressure-lowering effect, because you have eliminated the bacteria needed to complete the pathway. The remaining nitrite circulates systemically, and under low-oxygen conditions—precisely what working muscle experiences during high-intensity exercise—it is preferentially converted to NO exactly where it is needed most.

Top Nitrate-Rich Foods to Add to Your Diet

  • Beetroot and beet juice: 250–400 mg nitrate per 100g raw; the most studied food in performance research with consistent evidence across multiple sports
  • Arugula (rocket): Among the highest nitrate density of any common food, at 250–490 mg per 100g; easy to add to salads and sandwiches
  • Spinach: 65–250 mg per 100g; also rich in iron and folate, making it a nutritional double-duty vegetable
  • Swiss chard and kale: Moderate nitrate levels paired with broad micronutrient profiles that support the eNOS enzymatic pathway
  • Celery and radishes: Underrated nitrate sources, excellent for adding crunch to meals without significant calories
  • Romaine lettuce and fennel: Surprisingly high nitrate content means even regular salads contribute meaningfully to daily intake

What the Research Says About Athletic Performance

The evidence for dietary nitrate as an ergogenic aid is among the most robust in sports nutrition. A landmark University of Exeter study found that cyclists who drank 500 ml of beet juice daily for six days reduced oxygen consumption during moderate exercise by 19%—making their muscles measurably more fuel-efficient. Subsequent trials confirmed the effect across swimming, rowing, cross-country skiing, and team sports. A 2019 meta-analysis of 80 studies in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition found a consistent ~3% improvement in time-trial performance with dietary nitrate supplementation. That margin is meaningful at any level of competition. The mechanism is twofold: increased NO improves oxygen delivery through vasodilation and enhances the efficiency of Type II fast-twitch fibers, which are normally oxygen-costly and fatigue-prone. For everyday health, the cardiovascular benefits are equally compelling. A 2015 Queen Mary University of London randomized controlled trial showed that daily beet juice over four weeks lowered systolic blood pressure by 7.7 mmHg and diastolic by 2.4 mmHg in hypertensive adults—results that rival low-dose pharmacological intervention without a single side effect.

Study finding: In a landmark trial published in the Journal of Applied Physiology, cyclists consuming beet juice required 19% less oxygen to sustain the same exercise workload—an extraordinary metabolic efficiency gain from a single dietary change applied for just six days.

The Bottom Line

Nitric oxide is a master regulator of cardiovascular health, athletic performance, and metabolic function—and your plate is the most direct lever for its production. The practical takeaway is simpler than the biochemistry: eat more dark leafy greens and beets. An arugula salad, roasted beets on the side, or a daily handful of spinach can meaningfully shift your vascular biology within weeks. Skip antibacterial mouthwash right after eating these foods; you need those oral bacteria to complete the nitrate-to-NO conversion. Athletes should consider a standardized beet juice protocol (400–600 mg nitrate, 2–3 hours before activity) backed by the strongest evidence in the category.
